The board on Cyphal¶

With a CAN transceiver on the host link's pins, the board is a node on a Cyphal/CAN bus. It does what every Cyphal node does, and is a holo:

  • it gets a node-ID, by plug-and-play or from its settings;
  • it sends a heartbeat once a second (uavcan.node.Heartbeat);
  • it answers uavcan.node.GetInfo with its name, its version and its unique ID;
  • its settings are registers (uavcan.register.Access and List);
  • it restarts, resets or identifies itself when told (uavcan.node.ExecuteCommand);
  • it says which ports it uses every 10 seconds (uavcan.node.port.List);
  • and it is a holo: its motions, its light (the LED ring), direct moves of its axes, its state, its scenes, its screen, the LED ring's own settings, the board's events, the lists of its scenes and files, and its calibration, with the holo's own types (below).

The node is there to be found, identified, managed and driven, by a dome controller or by a tool such as cyphal_service or Yakut.

The bus is classic CAN, 1 Mbit/s unless changed. The USB console, Wi-Fi and the web app all work alongside it. UART, RS485 and CAN share the same pins, so only one of them runs at a time.

Turning it on¶

link mode can
restart

The node then asks the bus for a node-ID by plug-and-play. On a bus with no allocator, give it a fixed one first (cyphal id 52: any free one from 0 to 125 on your bus). link mode off (or another transport) and a restart turn it off again.

The node-ID¶

A node-ID is the board's address on the bus, from 0 to 127.

By plug-and-play, which is how it starts out. The board asks the bus for a node-ID, and an allocator on the bus gives it one. cyphal_service has an allocator, as does Yakut. The board asks at random intervals of 0.15 to 1 second, for as long as it takes, and sends nothing else meanwhile: with no allocator on the bus, the rest of the board carries on as usual and cyphal shows it waiting.

The allocator remembers which board it gave which node-ID, so a board gets the same one every time. The board itself doesn't save it. It asks again at every start, and runs on its node-ID a second or two after the start: never sooner than one, for the check it makes first. Against cyphal_service on the bench it was running 1.1 seconds after its start, and about 2 seconds after an allocator turned up on a bus where it had been waiting. It takes a node-ID from whichever node answers first: nothing in it expects a particular allocator.

This is the plug-and-play of Cyphal v1 for classic CAN: uavcan.pnp.NodeIDAllocationData.1.0, on subject 8166. A request has room for 48 bits of identity, not the whole unique ID, so the board asks with a pseudo-ID: the low 48 bits of the CRC-64/WE of its unique ID. cyphal shows it in hex and in decimal; allocators show one or the other (cyphal_service's pnp-list, decimal). It is fixed by the unique ID, so it doesn't change either. Cyphal's own tools send these requests without the CAN controller's automatic retransmission, so that two boards whose requests happen to share a CAN ID (one chance in 128) collide once rather than over and over; the board's CAN driver can't do that for some frames and not others, so its requests are retried until acknowledged, like every other frame it sends.

Fixed, for a bus with no allocator:

cyphal id 52
cyphal id auto

cyphal id auto goes back to plug-and-play. Either takes effect when the node next starts: cyphal restart, or a restart of the board. Node-IDs 126 and 127 are by convention left for diagnostic tools.

A node-ID that is already taken¶

Two nodes with one node-ID corrupt each other's transfers, so the board watches for it: a frame on the bus that carries the board's own node-ID can only have come from another node.

  • With a fixed node-ID, the node listens for 2 seconds before it sends anything. If it hears its node-ID in that time, it doesn't start.
  • With an allocated node-ID, the node listens from its start, all the while it asks, and doesn't take up the node-ID it is given until it has listened for at least a second, the longest a node may go between heartbeats, so that a node already using it has been heard. If the node-ID has been heard on the bus since the start, and in the last 3 seconds (the time after which a node that has gone quiet counts as gone), it doesn't start, and sends nothing as that node-ID. An allocator whose records were lost could hand out a node-ID that way.
  • Once running, with a fixed or an allocated node-ID, hearing its own node-ID stops it at once. It sends nothing more, not even a heartbeat. (A frame it had already handed to the CAN controller still goes; the node hands over one at a time, so that is one frame at most.)

Listening or stopped, the board stays on the bus as an ordinary CAN controller: it acknowledges the other nodes' frames, as every node on a bus must, and only its own sending stops.

Either way, the board logs a warning on the console:

W (5123) cyphal: node-ID 52 is in use by another node; Cyphal stopped

and cyphal shows why it stopped. The rest of the board is unaffected. The node stays stopped until it's given another node-ID or told to start again (cyphal restart), or the board restarts: it doesn't try again by itself, and a fixed node-ID never falls back to plug-and-play. An allocated one isn't asked for again either: the allocator would give the same node-ID. While stopped it still listens, so cyphal goes on counting the bus's traffic.

The board can only notice a node that is on the bus and sending. An allocator may have set a node-ID aside for a node that isn't there at the moment: the board can't know that, and a fixed node-ID that clashes with it runs until the other node turns up.

What it reports¶

Heartbeat, once a second: the time since the node started, in seconds, with its mode operational and its health nominal, or advisory while the holo can't move (health). It keeps time while the board is busy: the longest gap measured on the bench was 1.02 seconds, during a firmware update over Wi-Fi, and 1.01 seconds while a clip played with the LED ring running. Other nodes count a node as gone after 3 seconds without one.

GetInfo:

Field Value
name The node's name: r2.dome.holo.front unless changed
unique_id 16 bytes that identify this board, below
software_version The release's major and minor version (a pre-release such as v1.2.0-rc1 counts); 0.0 for a build that isn't a release
software_vcs_revision_id The commit a build between releases was made from: the short hash in its version (v1.1.0-2-gd9593bd gives 0xd9593bd). 0 in a release, whose version says which it is
hardware_version 1.0
protocol_version 1.0

The name¶

The name says what the node is. The default, r2.dome.holo.front, is for the front holoprojector of an R2 dome. A dome's other holos run the same firmware with another name:

cyphal name r2.dome.holo.top
cyphal name --reset

A controller can then find every holo by the start of its name, r2.dome.holo., and tell them apart by the end, with no table of node-IDs. Swapping the dots for slashes gives a name that works in ROS: /r2/dome/holo/front.

A name is 1 to 50 characters: lower-case letters, digits and _, in parts joined by .. A part can't be empty or start with a digit. Anything else is refused with the reason, and the name stays as it was:

cyphal: not a name: a part can't start with a digit: `1holo`

The new name is reported when the node next starts.

The unique ID¶

The allocator recognises a board by its unique ID, so it has to be the same after a firmware update, and after the board's settings are wiped. It is built from the chip's factory MAC address, which is burnt in and never changes:

Bytes Value
0–7 68 6f 6c 6f 70 6c 61 79: the letters holoplay, the same on every board
8–9 00 00
10–15 The chip's base MAC address. Its last two bytes are the xxxx of the board's holo-xxxx name

Replacing a board therefore changes the unique ID, and the new board gets a node-ID of its own from the allocator. To give it the old board's place on the bus, give it the old board's node-ID as a fixed one, or change the allocator's record.

Settings¶

Command Does
cyphal The node's state, its node-ID, name, unique ID and plug-and-play pseudo-ID, the bus's state, and the counters
cyphal id <0-127>\|auto A fixed node-ID, or plug-and-play. From the node's next start
cyphal name <name>\|--reset The name GetInfo reports. From the node's next start
cyphal bitrate <125k\|250k\|500k\|1m> The bus's bitrate. From the node's next start
cyphal restart Stop the node and start it again, with the settings as they now are
cyphal reset Back to the firmware's defaults: plug-and-play, the default name, 1 Mbit/s, every port's default ID
cyphal register [<name> [<value>…]] The same settings as Cyphal registers, with the holo's port numbers: all of them, one, or one written

The settings are saved, and they are part of a backup: the node-ID setting, the name, the bitrate and the port numbers (NVS namespace cyphal: node_id, 255 for plug-and-play; name; bitrate; and one for each port, pub.status to srv.media_info). A replacement board restored from a backup takes the old one's name and, if it was fixed, its node-ID. cyphal shows a setting changed since the node started on a line of its own, until cyphal restart.

The pins are the host link's: link pins changes them.

The first line starts with the node's state:

State Means
off The host link isn't running in CAN mode: link mode can, then restart
waiting for a node-ID Asking the bus for one by plug-and-play, and no allocator has answered yet; or one has, and the node is still in the second it listens before taking it up
listening, before going online as node 52 (fixed) The 2 seconds before a node with a fixed node-ID starts
node 52 (allocated) or (fixed) Running
stopped: node-ID 52 is in use by another node Stopped, until told to start again
not started on GPIO21 and GPIO33: … The CAN controller didn't start at the board's start (out of memory, say): the host link then holds no pins (link says can didn't start), and only a restart of the board tries again. Or a cyphal restart failed: the controller didn't start again, or would not stop, in which case the node is stopped and the controller still runs. cyphal restart tries again

The bus's state is CAN's own, read from the two error counters beside it (the larger of them):

State Means
error-active Normal: both counters under 96
error-warning A counter at 96 or more: errors lately, not yet enough to change how the controller behaves
error-passive A counter at 128 or more: many errors lately. A board alone on the bus sits here, with tx errors 128, since nobody acknowledges its frames
bus-off So many transmit errors that the controller has taken itself off the bus. The node brings it back by itself, and counts how often

Bus-off¶

A controller goes bus-off when its frames keep failing in a way that is its own fault: it sends a bit and doesn't see that bit on the bus. On a working bus that is rare, and the node brings the controller back within a second, with a warning in the board's log:

W (4663) cyphal: bus-off (1 times since the start): recovering

When the board can't see its own bits at all (the transceiver's S pin high, or its VIO, RXD or TXD not connected: what to check), every frame it sends takes it bus-off again within a moment of each recovery. So when a bus-off comes less than a minute after the recovery before it, the node waits before recovering: 1 s, then 2, 4, 8 and 16 s, and 30 s from then on, until the bus has stayed up for a minute. The log says so at the 2nd bus-off in a row, the 4th, the 8th, and so on, ever further apart:

W (5663) cyphal: bus-off again, 2 times in a row (2 since the start): recovering in 1 s; logged again at 4 in a row
W (10693) cyphal: bus-off again, 4 times in a row (4 since the start): recovering in 4 s; logged again at 8 in a row
W (72883) cyphal: bus-off again, 8 times in a row (8 since the start): recovering in 30 s; logged again at 16 in a row

and cyphal shows the wait:

  bus: bus-off, tx errors 128, rx errors 0, bus-off 7 times (back to back: recovering in 21 s)
  transfers: sent 7, received 0; frames: sent 0, received 0; dropped 41
  up 50 s; frames given to the controller 7, not sent 6, waiting 1; not read (queue full) 0

Each bus-off costs the frame that was on the wire (not sent); the heartbeats due meanwhile expire (dropped, about one a second). The rest of the board runs as usual.

The holo on Cyphal¶

A controller on the bus, the dome panel or an app on a Linux machine, can do with this holo what the panel does with a holo of its own: every motion, the light, direct moves of the axes, the holo's state, and letting go of the servos. It also reaches most of the board's own features: scenes, the screen, the LED ring, the board's events, and the lists of scenes and files. And the node is managed the standard Cyphal way, with registers and commands, by any Cyphal tool, the holo's calibration among them. The Cyphal interface reference has every type, port and register.

The holo's messages and services are Cyphal data types of their own, r2.holo.*, kept in the r2_cyphal_dsdl repository, which a controller generates its code from. Everything else (the heartbeat, GetInfo, registers, restart) uses Cyphal's standard types.

What a controller can do¶

To It Default port

Hear what happens: clips and scenes starting and ending, touches Subscribes to r2.holo.Event subject 2301

Start a motion Calls r2.holo.Motion service 100
Set the holo's light Calls r2.holo.Light service 101

Show a clip, an image or a colour, or nothing; set the backlight Calls r2.holo.Screen service 103
Set the LED ring Calls r2.holo.Ring service 104
List the saved scenes Calls r2.holo.ListScenes service 105
Ask what a clip or an image is Calls r2.holo.MediaInfo service 106

Each service does what the same request over the HTTP API or the host link does, through the same code, and refuses it for the same reasons: the answer carries the host link's status code (ok, bad request, not found, not playable, not ready, busy, failed) and the message in words, such as holo: refused, uncalibrated (tilt).

Moving the holo¶

r2.holo.Motion starts any of the holo's motions, with every parameter the console's holo takes: center, move, nudge, twitch, wag, nod, scan, circle, stop, and off, which stops, lets both servos go limp and turns the light (the ring) off. A parameter left at 0 takes the motion's default, as on the console. It runs the HTTP API's own POST /holo/motion, so a motion out of range is refused in the same words. A twitch or a scan takes a time limit, but a scan does not stop by it today, here or on the console or the HTTP API: a fault in the holo engine the board shares with the dome panel. Stop a scan with stop (or another motion) instead.

For a direct move, a controller streams setpoints instead: r2.holo.Axes messages with both axes in hundredths of a percent of their travel each side of the centre (−10000 to 10000). Each setpoint goes to the servos at once, with no easing, and stops any motion running first; the latest wins. This is what a joystick or a ROS joint command needs. The holo publishes where it has put the axes on the same scale, ten times a second while it moves and once a second at rest. On the bench, a 50-a-second stream reached the board whole, each setpoint at the servos at once, and stopped a twitch that was running.

A subject belongs to the whole bus, so two holos listening for setpoints on the same subject move together. Give a second holo its own setpoint subject by its register, uavcan.sub.setpoint.id.

The light is the LED ring¶

A dome panel's own holos have a single-colour light: off, on, pulse, flicker and blink, at a level, each for good or for a time, after which the light goes back to what it was doing; and leia, the message from Leia, a flicker at full with the holo turned to the centre. On this board r2.holo.Light does all of that on the LED ring: on lights it steady, and the others are ring modes of the same names. The ring keeps its colour unless the request gives one.

The level is a share of the ring's brightness, not a brightness of its own: the light at 100 is the ring at the brightness it is set to (leds bright, or the board's settings), so a controller can't light a ring powered from the board past its limit. The ring has pulse and blink, and timed modes, for this; the console's leds and the HTTP API have them too. The console's holo led and holo leia belong to the holo engine shared with the panel, and still do nothing on this board.

Knowing what it is doing¶

r2.holo.Status is one message with everything: the holo's motion (ready or why not, what runs, for how long, why the last one stopped early, the last motion asked for), the ring (including a timed mode and what it goes back to), the screen (what shows, a clip's progress, the backlight) and the scene running to its end. It goes out within a quarter of a second of a change, and once a second whatever changes.

r2.holo.Event carries the board's happenings, the same ones as its JSON events: a clip or a scene starting or ending, with its path or name, and a touch on the screen while touch reporting is on. Each is numbered with the board's sequence number, the one the web app and the host link see. There is no catching up on Cyphal: a controller that sees a number skipped has missed something, and reads the next Status for how things are now.

Scenes, the screen and the ring¶

r2.holo.Scene applies a saved scene by its slot or its name, or ends the one running to its end; r2.holo.Screen shows a clip, an image, a colour, the crosshair or nothing, and sets the backlight; r2.holo.Ring sets the LED ring's mode, colour, loop, period and brightness. Each runs the HTTP API's own code, so a missing file is refused as not found and one the screen can't show as not playable, in the same words. Showing a still decodes it, which takes up to a few hundred milliseconds: Scene and Screen requests are carried out one at a time by a task of their own, so the holo's setpoints and the board's Status never wait for them, and a controller waits for each response before it counts on the next request being done after it. The scenes themselves are made in the web app or with the HTTP API.

The lists¶

r2.holo.ListScenes gives the saved scenes one a call, by index, with their slots, in the order GET /api/v1/scenes has them. The standard uavcan.file.List and uavcan.file.GetInfo give the files on the board, as fs ls sees them: a directory's entries one a call, by index, in order of their names (a directory's with / after it), and each one's size and when it was last written. r2.holo.MediaInfo gives a clip's or an image's details from its header (its kind, size, frames, rate and length) and whether the screen can show it, and why not, as GET /api/v1/media/info does. All of them only read: files and scenes are made and changed over Wi-Fi. While the board is busy with earlier requests, uavcan.file.List isn't answered at all (its empty name would end the list), so a client asks again after its timeout. The reference has the details and the times on the bench.

Settings as registers¶

The node's settings are Cyphal registers, which any Cyphal tool lists, reads and writes:

Register The setting Default
uavcan.node.id The node-ID, 0–127; 65535 for plug-and-play (cyphal id) 65535
r2.node.name The name GetInfo reports (cyphal name) r2.dome.holo.front
uavcan.can.bitrate The bitrate (cyphal bitrate) 1000000
uavcan.pub.status.id, … uavcan.srv.ring.id, … Each port's number; 65535 turns the port off The defaults above
r2.holo.horizontal.endpoints_us, r2.holo.vertical.endpoints_us Each axis's closed and open ends in µs (holo endpoints); 0 0 clears them The default range, not saved
r2.holo.horizontal.release, …vertical.release, …settle_ms Each servo's drive policy (the web app's, PUT /api/v1/servos/policy) Hold everywhere, 100 ms
r2.touch.reporting Touch events on or off, saved: the board's touch setting (settings touch; touch on and touch off don't save) Off

They are the same saved settings the cyphal commands change, and the console reads and writes the registers by name too:

cyphal register
cyphal register uavcan.sub.setpoint.id
cyphal register uavcan.sub.setpoint.id 2313

uavcan.sub.setpoint.id          2313                   natural16[1], mutable, persistent; 2303 until the node's next start

A change takes effect when the node next starts (cyphal restart). A value the board can't take is refused with the reason, and the register keeps its value:

cyphal: uavcan.sub.setpoint.id not written: a subject-ID is 0 to 8191, or 65535 to turn the port off

A number another port already has is refused as well, with the port that has it: the subjects, published or subscribed, are one set and the services another, and the node's own standard ports count (the rule). To swap two ports' numbers, move one to a free number, or to 65535, first.

Over Cyphal the response says the same by carrying the old value.

The holo's calibration registers take effect at once, and are the servos' own saved values, the ones holo endpoints, the web app's calibration and PUT /api/v1/servos/policy change: a change either way shows the other way, at once. Writing 0 0 to an axis's endpoints clears its drive policy too, as holo endpoints <axis> clear does. Touch reporting is one of the board's settings (settings touch, touch.reporting in /api/v1/settings): saved, applied at once and at every start; touch on and touch off change it until the next start, and the register reads what is in effect.

The port numbers are saved and go into a backup with the rest of the node's settings. A second holo restored from the same backup needs its own node-ID and its own setpoint subject.

Restart, factory reset, identify¶

uavcan.node.ExecuteCommand, the standard command service:

  • restart restarts the board, a second after it answers;
  • factory reset puts the node's registers and the board's settings (touch reporting among them) back to their defaults and restarts, keeping the scenes, the files, the servos' calibration and drive policy, the network settings and the host link's mode (so the node comes back on the bus, asking for a node-ID by plug-and-play);
  • identify blinks the ring white and shows the crosshair for 5 seconds, to tell which board on the bus is which, then puts both back. It runs as a short scene called identify, so the web app shows it, and it ends a scene that was running.

Every other command, power off and emergency stop among them, is refused as a bad command. The board's log says which node asked.

From a computer¶

Yakut speaks all of this, given the standard types and r2_cyphal_dsdl on its CYPHAL_PATH: the folders that hold uavcan and r2. With the board at node-ID 52, it would read a register, start a wag, and restart the board:

export CYPHAL_PATH="$HOME/public_regulated_data_types:$HOME/r2_cyphal_dsdl"
yakut register-access 52 uavcan.srv.motion.id
yakut call 52 motion '{motion: {kind: 4, count: 3}}'
yakut execute-command 52 restart

On macOS a gs_usb adapter (a CANable, candleLight) needs a workaround first: python-can, which Yakut uses, asks the system to detach a kernel driver, and macOS refuses without root. This repository's tools/yakut_can.py runs Yakut with it, and with the adapter opened and closed only while the board is quiet, which a candleLight needs; it sets the transport and CYPHAL_PATH from the repository's own copies of the types, and wants the board on a fixed node-ID:

tools/yakut_can.py register-list 52
tools/yakut_can.py register-access 52 uavcan.srv.motion.id
tools/yakut_can.py register-access 52 uavcan.pub.status.id 2310

It refuses execute-command (which newer versions of the click library name execute), and a call to uavcan.node.ExecuteCommand: on the bench, with the adapter's earlier firmware (a canable.io build of candleLight, until 2026-11-21), the candleLight now and then stopped passing frames to the computer during a Yakut session, every time a command was sent and sometimes otherwise, and only the adapter's own bootloader brought it back. Its firmware has since been replaced by a build of candleLight's current main branch, on which Yakut hasn't been tried; the refusal stays until it has. The repository's hardware tests send the commands instead. It also refuses what would leave the board never quiet when the adapter has to close (a write of 65535, plug-and-play, to uavcan.node.id: use cyphal id auto on the console), register-batch, and a --bitrate other than the board's.

What stays off Cyphal¶

Uploading clips and images, the network and the web app's settings, passwords, the configuration backup and firmware updates stay on Wi-Fi and the USB console. So do saving and editing scenes: Cyphal applies a saved one. The reference has the whole list.

transfers counts what was sent, whole (heartbeats, port lists, responses and plug-and-play requests) and what was received (requests and plug-and-play messages); frames counts what the controller sent and had acknowledged, and what it received. dropped counts transfers the board gave up on because the bus didn't take them in time (a second, a heartbeat's period), or that it couldn't queue or put together at all. It rises while the board is alone on the bus, about once a second as each heartbeat expires (a little less while it waits for a node-ID: each request expires in turn, and the next isn't queued until it has), and shouldn't otherwise. The frame the controller was retrying then goes out when another node turns up, a little late.

The next two lines are for when something seems wrong: how long the node has run, frames it handed to the controller that were never sent (bus-off, or a restart of the node), frames that arrived faster than they could be read, the most frames its transmit queue has held since the start (of the 160 it can), and the memory it uses. Its libcanard heap is 32 KB of PSRAM, reserved at its first start; a port list waiting to go takes about 1.8 KB of it. The internal RAM it takes is its task's 3 KB stack while it runs, the worker's 4.5 KB stack (the task that writes registers and reads the holo's, carries out the holo's Motion, Light, Ring and ListScenes requests and its setpoints, and builds what the holo publishes, so that the node's own never waits) from its first start on, the second worker's 7.5 KB stack (the task that carries out Scene, Screen and MediaInfo requests, the file services and identify, which may decode an image, read a file whole or wait for the storage volume; the host link's own task, which runs the same routes on 10 KB, doesn't run while the node has its pins), and the CAN driver's queues (about 8 KB, kept once the controller has run, the same as for can). The holo's mailboxes and the worker's copy of Status are in PSRAM.

services counts the register and command requests, the ones refused, and those answered busy because the worker already held four, or the second worker two, or left unanswered for that reason (a read of one of the holo's servo registers, a uavcan.file.List: their types can't say busy, and the client asks again); worker shows how many each holds now. application counts the holo's own: requests to its services handed to the workers and setpoints received, and gives the health the heartbeat reports. published counts each message the holo publishes, Status, Event and Axes, that was queued for the bus, and in brackets, for Status and Axes, those a newer one replaced before they went (none, on a quiet bus), and for Event, those its full queue refused (each offer refused: the Event is offered again a moment later, and the happenings after it are read from the board's record once it has gone). A message waits in its mailbox until the transmit queue has room for the whole of it; one queued that then waited there past its second is counted with the transfers dropped.

Testing the wiring: raw frames¶

The can command works below Cyphal: plain CAN frames, for checking a transceiver and a bus. It uses the same pins and the same controller, so it runs only while the Cyphal node is off (link mode off), and the node won't start while it's running. While the node holds the controller, can alone still reports the bus (its first line ends for the Cyphal node), and every can sub-command refuses:

can: the controller is in use by the Cyphal node: `can` only reports while it is
Command Does
can Whether it's running, the bus's state and error counters, and frames sent and received
can start [-b <bitrate>] [--listen\|--selftest] Start the controller, at 1 Mbit/s unless told otherwise. --listen receives without sending or acknowledging. --selftest receives its own frames and needs no acknowledgement
can stop Stop, and release the pins as floating inputs. dump and echo go off, so the next can start is quiet
can send <id>#<hex> Send one frame. An ID of up to three hex digits is an 11-bit one; eight digits, a 29-bit one. Up to 8 data bytes
can dump [on\|off] Print every frame received, until turned off (on if neither is given)
can echo [on\|off] Send every frame received straight back, with 1 added to its ID
can recover Bring the controller back from bus-off

With no second node, a self-test shows that the controller, the two pins and the transceiver work:

can start --selftest
can dump on
can send 123#DEADBEEF

The frame comes back:

can: 123 [4] DE AD BE EF

can stop ends the self-test. A board that can't see its own bits on the bus fails it: the frame never comes back, and the controller goes bus-off at once (can reads bus-off, bus-off 1 times, waiting to send 1). The transceiver's S pin high (silent), or its VIO, RXD or TXD not connected, does that.

With an adapter on the bus, can start and can dump on show what it sends, and can send 1ABCDEF0#0102 should arrive there. A frame that is sent but never acknowledged leaves tx errors climbing: a first test lists what to check.

can alone reports:

can: running at 1 Mbit/s on GPIO21 (TX), GPIO33 (RX), normal
  bus: error-active, tx errors 0, rx errors 0, bus errors 0, bus-off 0 times
  frames: sent 4, received 1000; send failed 0, receive dropped 0 (queue held 5 of 256 at most), waiting to send 0
  dump off, echo off; internal heap 107259 bytes free, 69388 lowest

bus errors counts every error the controller has seen on the bus since can start, not counting lost arbitration: thousands a second while nobody acknowledges, since each retry of the frame is one. send failed counts frames that never made it: the one on the wire when the controller went bus-off, and any still waiting at can stop. While the controller is bus-off, can send refuses (can: bus-off: `can recover` first); frames already waiting behind the bus-off go out after can recover, which brings the controller back once the bus has been idle for 128 periods of 11 bits. Unlike the node, can never recovers by itself. receive dropped counts frames that arrived faster than the board could take them, which can dump causes on a busy bus: printing is slower than CAN. The queue between the controller and the board's reader holds 256 frames, enough to ride out a flash write on a bus full of 8-byte frames; queue held says how full it has been since can start. With --listen, the bus always reads error-passive with rx errors 128: that keeps the controller from ever sending an error flag. While can runs, gpio refuses its two pins.

tools/can_check.py, in this repository, is the computer's side, through a gs_usb adapter (candleLight, CANable): listen, send, flood, and echo, which sends numbered frames to a board running can echo on and checks that every one comes back, in order. An adapter keeps retrying a frame nobody acknowledged, even after it is closed and opened again; can start on the board acknowledges it and clears it.
